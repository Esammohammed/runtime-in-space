# Isolation and confinement

Source: NASA Human Research Program, "The Five Hazards of Human Spaceflight",
the "Behavioral Health and Performance" risk summary, and the Artemis II
ARCHeR actigraphy study on crew sleep and activity (https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/).

A long mission puts a crew in a small, enclosed space with the same few people
for months at a time, far from family, with limited privacy and a communication
delay back to Earth that gets longer the farther the vehicle travels. NASA
groups the resulting risks under behavioral health: mood changes, interpersonal
friction within the crew, and a drop in motivation or performance that can build
up gradually rather than appearing as a single event.

Sleep is one of the clearest and most measurable warning signs. The demands of a
mission schedule, the absence of a normal day-night cycle, and the stress of
confinement all tend to push astronauts toward shorter, more disrupted sleep.
NASA's Human Research Program studies this directly through actigraphy -- wrist-
worn activity sensors that estimate sleep quality and duration without needing a
full sleep lab. Persistent short sleep is treated as an early sign of the wider
behavioral health risk, not just a comfort issue.

Because there is no quick medical fix for isolation itself, the practical
response on board is routine and connection: protect sleep, keep to a regular
schedule, and flag a sustained mood or sleep decline early so the crew medical
officer can check in before it affects judgment or teamwork. This software
treats a drifting mood or sleep reading the same way -- an early nudge to check
in, not a diagnosis.
