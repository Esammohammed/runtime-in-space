# Altered gravity fields

Source: NASA Human Research Program, "The Five Hazards of Human Spaceflight"
and the "Human Health Countermeasures" risk summaries on cardiovascular
adaptation and reduced fitness (https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/).

The human body evolved under one constant gravity field, and it changes
quickly when that field is removed, reduced, or changed again on arrival at a
destination. On the way to a destination like the Moon or Mars, a crew moves
from Earth gravity to weightlessness and then to a different, lower gravity --
each transition asks the body to re-adapt.

The cardiovascular system is one of the clearest examples. On Earth, the heart
and blood vessels work continuously against gravity to keep blood from pooling
in the legs. In microgravity that workload drops, and the cardiovascular system
adapts to the new, lower demand -- a change that can affect heart rate and
blood pressure and that reverses only gradually after returning to gravity.
Reduced physical loading during a mission can also contribute to lower overall
fitness, though that effect is addressed mainly through exercise countermeasures
and routine rather than through any single measurement.

Because the underlying cause is the gravity environment itself, there is no
on-board treatment that removes the risk -- the standard response is scheduled
exercise and monitoring heart rate and blood pressure for a drift away from an
astronaut's own normal range. This software treats a drifting heart rate or
blood pressure reading as a sign to check in on exercise routine and rest, not
as an emergency on its own, unless the reading is far outside the safe range.
