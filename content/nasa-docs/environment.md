# Hostile and closed environments

Source: NASA Human Research Program, "The Five Hazards of Human Spaceflight"
and the risk summaries on immune dysregulation, cabin atmosphere, and
nutrition (https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/).

A spacecraft or station is a small, sealed box that has to recycle its own air
and water and carry its own food for months at a time. NASA groups several
separate risks under this hazard because they all come from the same root
cause: there is no outside environment to fall back on if something inside
drifts out of balance.

Cabin atmosphere is one part of this. Carbon dioxide builds up continuously
from the crew's own breathing and has to be actively scrubbed from the air;
if the scrubbing system falls behind, CO2 levels can rise enough to cause
headaches, fatigue, and reduced thinking clearly well before oxygen itself
becomes a problem. Blood oxygen is the complementary signal on the body side --
a drop there can reflect either a cabin atmosphere problem or a change in the
astronaut's own respiratory health.

Spaceflight also changes how the immune system behaves, for reasons that are
still an active area of NASA research -- some latent viruses that are normally
kept suppressed can reactivate, and wound healing or skin reactions can look or
behave differently than they would on Earth. Because food and nutrition are
also part of this closed system, a limited and unchanging diet over a long
mission is tracked as a contributor to the same broad risk category.

Because the cause is the environment itself, the practical response on board is
monitoring and maintenance: watch cabin CO2 and blood oxygen for a drift, watch
skin or wound changes that do not resolve as expected, and treat a sustained
drift in any of these as a signal to check the life-support systems and alert
the crew medical officer, not something to wait out.
