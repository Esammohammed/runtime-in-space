# Radiation

Source: NASA Human Research Program, "The Five Hazards of Human Spaceflight" and
"Risks of Human Spaceflight" (https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/), plus the Canadian Space
Agency's work on radiation and the CANS (Cognitive and Neurological effects)
research line (https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/).

Space radiation is different from radiation on Earth. Outside the protection of
Earth's atmosphere and magnetic field, astronauts are exposed to galactic cosmic
rays that arrive constantly from outside the solar system, and to solar particle
events thrown out by the Sun during flares and coronal mass ejections. Both kinds
of radiation are more energetic and harder to shield against than the radiation
sources common on Earth.

The main long-term concern NASA tracks is an increased lifetime risk of cancer
from accumulated radiation dose. Because the risk builds up over a career rather
than appearing immediately, NASA tracks each astronaut's cumulative exposure
across all of their missions, not just the current one. A single mission's
average daily dose is usually manageable; the danger is a large spike during an
unshielded solar particle event, which is why crews are trained to move to a
shielded part of the vehicle or station when a solar event is flagged.

There is no quick fix once a high dose has been received. The practical response
on board is prevention and shelter: track the dose, flag any value drifting
upward, and move to a shielded location immediately if a solar particle event is
suspected. This software treats a rising radiation reading as an early signal to
check shelter status and alert the crew medical officer, not as something a crew
member can treat with a drug or a daily routine change.
